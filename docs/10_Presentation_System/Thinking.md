## Purpose

Presentation Thinking defines how LINKGENT transforms information into shared understanding, decision, and action before visual production begins.

This is the most important file in the Presentation System.

Layout, typography, color, components, and templates are implementation layers. They cannot compensate for unclear purpose, weak information architecture, or an unsupported message.

```text
Think
  ↓
Structure
  ↓
Design
  ↓
Communicate
```

The presentation process must never begin with:

- Choosing a template
- Selecting a color palette
- Arranging boxes
- Searching for icons
- Writing slide titles without a story
- Generating layouts with AI

It begins with the audience and the decision the communication must enable.

---

## Core Definition

A presentation is a designed sequence of understanding.

It should help the audience answer, in order:

1. Why does this matter?
2. What should I understand?
3. What evidence supports it?
4. What changes because of it?
5. What decision or action is required?

The goal is not to transfer every available piece of information.

The goal is to structure the necessary information so the audience can form the intended understanding with the lowest reasonable cognitive burden.

## Audience First

### Principle

The presentation is designed for the audience's understanding, not the presenter's convenience.

Audience First means understanding the people who must receive, interpret, evaluate, remember, or act on the information.

### Required Audience Definition

Before structuring the deck, define:

- Who the primary audience is
- Who may influence the decision
- Who owns the final decision
- What the audience already knows
- What the audience does not know
- What assumptions they may hold
- What matters to them
- What risks or objections they may perceive
- What language and level of detail they can use
- How much time and attention are available
- Whether the presentation is live, read independently, or both
- What accessibility and viewing conditions apply

### Audience Layers

A deck may serve several audience layers, but one must be primary.

| Audience Layer | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Primary | Must understand the core message and make or support the intended decision |
| Secondary | Needs enough context to align, advise, or execute |
| Reference | May use detailed evidence, methodology, or appendix content later |

Do not force all audience needs into every slide.

Use the main story for the primary audience and place specialist or reference detail where it can be accessed without disrupting the main communication path.

### Audience Test

A presentation fails Audience First when:

- It uses internal language the audience does not understand
- It explains what the presenter knows instead of what the audience needs
- It assumes context that has not been established
- It provides detail before relevance
- It asks for a decision without defining the decision criteria
- It is readable only from the presenter's screen
- It depends on spoken explanation when it will be read independently

## Purpose First

### Principle

Every presentation must have one primary communication purpose.

A deck may support several outcomes, but the primary purpose governs story, density, evidence, pacing, and ending.

### Common Purposes

| Purpose | Intended Outcome |
|---|---|
| Inform | Establish accurate shared knowledge |
| Explain | Make a mechanism, relationship, or reason understandable |
| Align | Create a shared interpretation, direction, or priority |
| Decide | Enable a choice, approval, or prioritization |
| Persuade | Change belief or increase confidence through reason and evidence |
| Teach | Build understanding or capability over time |
| Propose | Present a recommended response to a defined need |
| Mobilize | Move people toward coordinated action |

### Purpose Statement

Write the purpose as an audience outcome, not a production task.

Preferred:

> Enable the executive team to decide whether to fund the proposed service redesign.

Avoid:

> Create a 20-slide proposal deck.

### Purpose Test

The purpose is sufficiently clear when it defines:

```text
Audience
+ desired understanding
+ required decision or action
+ relevant context
```

If the purpose cannot be stated in one sentence, the deck scope is probably unresolved.

## Message Architecture

### Deck Thesis

Every deck must have one central thesis.

The thesis is the most important conclusion the audience should retain after the presentation.

It should be:

- Specific
- Relevant to the audience
- Supported by evidence
- Actionable or decision-relevant
- Distinguishable from a topic label

Topic:

> Customer support transformation

Thesis:

> Redesigning the first-contact experience will reduce avoidable support demand while improving customer confidence.

### Message Hierarchy

```text
Deck Thesis
  ↓
Chapter Messages
  ↓
Slide Messages
  ↓
Evidence and Explanation
  ↓
Detail and Sources
```

Every slide message must contribute to a chapter message.

Every chapter message must contribute to the deck thesis.

If a slide does not support the thesis, provide necessary context, manage a material objection, or enable the intended action, remove it or move it to an appendix.

### Claim, Evidence, Implication, Action

A strong presentation distinguishes four information roles:

| Role | Question |
|---|---|
| Claim | What should the audience understand? |
| Evidence | Why should they believe it? |
| Implication | Why does it matter? |
| Action | What should happen next? |

Not every slide requires all four roles, but the deck must make their relationship clear.

## One Slide, One Message

### Principle

Each slide should communicate one primary message.

One Slide, One Message does not mean one sentence, one object, or one data point.

It means that all content on the slide should work together to establish one conclusion.

### Message Title

When appropriate, the slide title should express the conclusion rather than merely name the topic.

Preferred:

> Most abandonment occurs before users see relevant search results.

Avoid:

> Search Analysis

Topic titles may be used for section dividers, agendas, and reference pages. Analytical and persuasive slides should normally use message-led titles.

### Slide Test

A slide passes when the audience can answer within a few seconds:

- What is this slide saying?
- What should I look at first?
- What evidence matters?
- Why is this included now?

A slide fails when:

- It contains several unrelated conclusions
- The title does not match the evidence
- Every element competes for attention
- The audience must read all details before identifying the point
- The slide exists only because the content was available

### Split Before Shrink

When content does not fit:

1. Remove content that does not support the message.
2. Rewrite and simplify.
3. Group related information.
4. Move reference detail to notes or appendix.
5. Split the content into a sequence of messages.
6. Change the representation.
7. Reduce type size only when the content role legitimately requires a smaller approved typography token.

Never solve unresolved information architecture by compressing everything.

## Information Architecture

### Principle

Information architecture determines what information belongs, how it is grouped, how it is prioritized, and how relationships are made visible.

Simplicity is not the absence of information.

It is the result of strong structure.

### Information Architecture Process

#### 1. Inventory

Identify all candidate information:

- Facts
- Claims
- Evidence
- Definitions
- Constraints
- Risks
- Questions
- Decisions
- Actions
- Sources

Do not assume all available information belongs in the main deck.

#### 2. Normalize

Resolve:

- Duplicate ideas
- Inconsistent terminology
- Conflicting numbers
- Unequal levels of detail
- Mixed time periods
- Mixed units
- Unsupported assumptions

#### 3. Classify

Assign each item a role:

```text
Essential
Supporting
Reference
Remove
```

#### 4. Group

Group by meaning, not by visual convenience.

Valid grouping principles include:

- Shared purpose
- Shared audience question
- Common stage
- Common category
- Cause and effect
- Part and whole
- Current and future state
- Option and criterion
- Evidence and conclusion

#### 5. Prioritize

Determine:

- What must be understood first
- What must be remembered
- What requires evidence
- What can be deferred
- What belongs in the appendix

#### 6. Map Relationships

Identify whether the information expresses:

- Hierarchy
- Sequence
- Comparison
- Causality
- Dependency
- Flow
- Change over time
- Distribution
- Composition
- Network
- Spatial relationship

The relationship determines the representation.

### Information Roles on a Slide

A slide should establish clear semantic roles:

- Primary message
- Primary evidence or visual
- Supporting explanation
- Context or qualifier
- Source or metadata
- Action, when required

Do not make supporting information visually equivalent to the primary message.

## Story Design

### Principle

Story is the order in which understanding develops.

It is not theatrical decoration. It is the deliberate sequencing of information so each message prepares the audience for the next.

### Story Questions

A strong story answers:

1. What is the current context?
2. What is changing, failing, or becoming possible?
3. What evidence reveals the real issue or opportunity?
4. What does that evidence mean?
5. What response is recommended?
6. What trade-offs, risks, or requirements exist?
7. What decision or action follows?

### Core Story Pattern

```text
Context
  ↓
Tension or Opportunity
  ↓
Insight
  ↓
Response
  ↓
Evidence and Feasibility
  ↓
Decision
  ↓
Action
```

This is a flexible reasoning pattern, not a mandatory slide sequence.

### Story Variants

#### Decision Story

```text
Decision required
  → criteria
  → options
  → evidence
  → trade-offs
  → recommendation
  → next action
```

#### Problem–Solution Story

```text
Current state
  → problem
  → root cause
  → impact
  → solution
  → expected value
  → implementation
```

#### Insight Story

```text
Question
  → observation
  → pattern
  → interpretation
  → implication
  → response
```

#### Progress Story

```text
Goal
  → baseline
  → progress
  → variance
  → causes
  → risks
  → next priorities
```

### Narrative Continuity

Adjacent slides should have a clear relationship.

Each transition should be explainable with one of the following:

- Therefore
- However
- Because
- For example
- Compared with
- Next
- As a result
- To decide this

If no relationship exists, the story may be a collection rather than an argument.

### Story Integrity

Do not:

- Hide material risks to create a cleaner narrative
- Present correlation as causation
- Place recommendations before establishing the problem or criteria
- Use emotional drama as a substitute for evidence
- Force every subject into a problem–solution pattern
- Repeat the same conclusion across several slides without adding meaning

## Information Density

### Principle

Density is determined by audience, purpose, mode, complexity, and viewing conditions.

Minimal does not mean empty.

Detailed does not mean dense.

A slide is too dense when the audience cannot identify the message, relationships, and required interpretation within the available time.

### Density Modes

| Mode | Appropriate Use | Typical Composition |
|---|---|---|
| Statement | Opening, transition, major conclusion | One message with minimal support |
| Standard | Most live presentation slides | One message with two to four structured supporting groups |
| Analytical | Data, comparison, process, evidence | One message with one primary analytical object and focused annotation |
| Reference | Read-ahead, appendix, documentation | Higher detail with explicit structure, labels, and sources |

### Density Budget

Every slide has limited attention capacity.

Visual weight is consumed by:

- Text volume
- Number of groups
- Number of data series
- Number of colors
- Number of shapes
- Number of alignment anchors
- Image complexity
- Annotation count
- Interaction or animation

When one element becomes complex, simplify the others.

Examples:

- A complex chart requires a simple title, limited color, and focused annotation.
- A dense comparison table requires restrained surrounding content.
- A strong image requires minimal overlaid text.

### Density Review

Ask:

- Is every element necessary for the slide message?
- Is the primary message visible before details?
- Can content be grouped more meaningfully?
- Is detail in the correct mode or should it move to an appendix?
- Is the slide intended to be seen, read, or analyzed?
- Can the presenter realistically explain the slide in the allocated time?

## Diagram First

### Principle

When the meaning depends on relationships, show the relationships visually before describing them in prose.

Use a diagram when the audience must understand:

- Sequence
- Process
- Hierarchy
- System structure
- Dependency
- Cause and effect
- Flow
- Transformation
- Comparison
- Change over time
- Part-to-whole relationships

### Diagram Decision

```text
Is the core meaning relational?
  ├── No → Use concise text, metric, image, or table.
  └── Yes
       ↓
Can the relationship be represented accurately?
  ├── No → Clarify the information first.
  └── Yes → Select the simplest valid diagram type.
```

### Diagram Rules

- Start from the relationship, not the available shape library.
- Use the minimum number of nodes and connectors required.
- Make direction explicit.
- Label relationships when direction alone is insufficient.
- Use alignment and proximity before color.
- Use Accent only for the focal node, step, or connection.
- Preserve a readable path without crossing lines where possible.
- Do not use decorative diagrams for information that is clearer as text.

### Diagram Integrity

A diagram must not imply:

- Sequence where none exists
- Equality between unequal items
- Causality without evidence
- Precision not supported by data
- Completeness when items are illustrative

## Comparison Before Decoration

### Principle

When a message depends on difference, make the comparison explicit before adding visual expression.

A comparison must define:

- The items being compared
- The shared dimensions
- The baseline
- The units
- The time period
- The order
- The meaningful difference
- The implication

### Comparison Quality

Preferred:

- Shared scales
- Consistent units
- Direct labels
- Aligned rows or axes
- Comparable wording
- Explicit criteria
- A clear focal difference

Avoid:

- Unequal visual scales
- Different units without explanation
- Lists that appear comparable but use different dimensions
- Color-only distinction
- Decorative cards that hide the basis of comparison
- Before/after claims without a stable baseline

### Comparison Sequence

```text
Define the decision
  ↓
Define the comparison criteria
  ↓
Normalize the evidence
  ↓
Show similarities and differences
  ↓
Interpret the trade-offs
  ↓
State the recommendation or implication
```

## Visual Hierarchy

### Principle

The intended reading order should be visible before the audience reads every word.

Hierarchy is created through meaningfully controlled differences in:

1. Position
2. Grouping
3. Size
4. Weight
5. Spacing
6. Contrast
7. Color
8. Detail

Decoration is not a hierarchy method.

### Hierarchy Roles

A standard slide should establish:

```text
Primary message
  ↓
Primary visual or evidence
  ↓
Supporting explanation
  ↓
Metadata and source
```

Use no more hierarchy levels than the message requires.

The Typography System recommends a maximum of three visible hierarchy levels per slide. Additional metadata may exist but must remain clearly subordinate.

### Emphasis Rule

One slide should normally have one dominant focal point.

Multiple elements may support the focal point. They should not compete with it.

Use emphasis in this order:

```text
Structure
  → Position
  → Scale
  → Weight
  → Spacing
  → Color
  → Decorative treatment only when justified
```

## Design Balance

### Principle

A successful presentation balances logic, communication, visual quality, and human attention.

### Four Forms of Balance

#### Logical Balance

Claims, evidence, qualifications, risks, and recommendations are proportionate.

Do not overstate the conclusion relative to the evidence.

#### Informational Balance

The audience receives enough context to understand, but not so much that the main message disappears.

#### Visual Balance

Visual weight is distributed intentionally across the canvas.

Visual weight increases through:

- Larger size
- Darker value
- Stronger saturation
- Higher contrast
- Greater density
- Image detail
- Isolation
- Unusual shape or position

Balance does not require symmetry.

#### Human Balance

The presentation respects attention, emotion, uncertainty, and decision pressure.

Human-centered communication is calm, clear, honest, and appropriately paced.

### Minimal and Beautiful

Minimal means:

- No unnecessary information
- No unnecessary visual language
- No unnecessary variation
- No unnecessary interaction

Minimal does not mean:

- Small type
- Weak contrast
- Empty slides without meaning
- Removal of necessary evidence
- Generic visual sameness

Beautiful means that structure, meaning, and expression feel coherent.

## Evidence and Trust

LINKGENT presentations must distinguish:

- Fact
- Interpretation
- Assumption
- Estimate
- Proposal
- Decision

Sources, time periods, units, and methods must be visible when they materially affect interpretation.

Do not fabricate data, quotes, customer statements, sources, or certainty.

When evidence is incomplete:

- State the limitation
- Mark the item as an assumption or estimate
- Explain what validation is required
- Avoid visual precision that exceeds the evidence

Reliability is part of visual quality.

## AI Thinking Process

AI must follow the same hierarchy as a human presentation designer.

```text
1. Audience
2. Purpose
3. Key Message
4. Story
5. Information Architecture
6. Information Density
7. Layout
8. Components
9. Visual Design
10. Review
```

### Required AI Behavior

AI must:

- Read the relevant Brand OS sources first
- Separate defined facts from inferred structure
- Identify missing evidence and unresolved decisions
- Draft the deck thesis before slide titles
- Draft the story before layout
- Define each slide message before selecting components
- Select representation from the information relationship
- Use semantic typography and color roles
- Preserve editability and traceability
- Review the complete deck, not only individual slides

AI must not:

- Start from a template gallery
- Generate slides directly from raw notes without structuring them
- Use a different visual pattern for every slide
- Add icons, gradients, illustrations, or imagery to fill space
- Invent numerical evidence or sources
- make all slides equally dense
- Treat a visually polished output as complete without logic review

Detailed AI rules are defined in [`AI_Generation.md`](./AI_Generation.md).

## Thinking Workflow

### Phase 1: Define

- Identify the audience
- Define the purpose
- Define the decision or action
- Define presentation mode and constraints

### Phase 2: Structure

- Inventory and normalize information
- Write the deck thesis
- Define chapter messages
- Classify essential, supporting, reference, and removable content

### Phase 3: Story

- Sequence the reasoning
- Validate transitions
- Identify evidence gaps and objections
- Define the ending before producing slides

### Phase 4: Specify Slides

For each slide, define:

- Purpose in the story
- One primary message
- Evidence or explanation
- Information relationship
- Density mode
- Appropriate component or representation

### Phase 5: Design

- Apply layout
- Apply typography hierarchy
- Apply semantic color
- Add imagery or iconography only when it improves understanding

### Phase 6: Review

- Review logic
- Review audience comprehension
- Review story continuity
- Review density
- Review accessibility
- Review brand alignment
- Review decision readiness

Use [`Checklist.md`](./Checklist.md) as the final quality gate.
